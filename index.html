Optics module

New in 1.1: see how your stars' blur changes across the frame - for collimation, tilt and focus. The Optics module reads the blur from your stars and separates it into its parts - round blur (seeing, guiding and optics), defocus, astigmatism and coma - and shows each one on its own map across the frame, either as a colour plot or as a 3D surface you can turn, together with the stars' measured size and real stars beside the model's. It also estimates where the optical centre lies - the point the aberrations grow away from, often not the middle of the frame - and can mark it on every plot. Load a linear image, tell StellAcuity about your telescope, and press Map the optics: a few seconds later every map is on screen at once. Nothing in your image is changed.

M56 with a 12" RC: the stars grow and stretch towards the right-hand edge, and the optical centre lies left of the middle.

Questions

Which images work best?

Linear (unstretched) stacks, before any stretching or other sharpening. For the best results, work in this order:

  1. Crop away the ragged stacking edges.
  2. Remove the gradient, leaving a small positive background rather than clipping it to zero.
  3. Colour calibrate colour images, keeping the data linear.
  4. Sharpen with StellAcuity.
  5. Remove stars with StellAcuity, if you want a starless image (and the stars on their own).
  6. Reduce noise with StellAcuity.
  7. Stretch and carry on with your usual creative processing.

Already have a stretched image, such as a JPEG or a 16-bit TIFF? Reduce noise works well on those too. Sharpen is meant for linear data and may refuse a strongly stretched image, because stretching changes the shape of the stars it measures.

How do I put the stars back after removing them?

Save the stars image as well (step 6). The starless image and the stars image add up to your image exactly, so you can stretch and process the starless image on its own and then add the stars back - in PixInsight, for example, with PixelMath: starless + stars while both are still linear. Stretched separately, they are usually put back together with a screen blend instead.

Do I need to enter my telescope, camera or pixel scale?

Not to sharpen, remove stars or reduce noise: StellAcuity measures the star shape across the image from its own stars. The Optics module needs to know a little about your telescope - see below.

What does the Optics module need?

A linear (unstretched) image with a few hundred stars or more - a stacked master is ideal. It needs the pixel scale and your telescope's type, aperture and filter; StellAcuity fills these in from the file when the file records them, and remembers what you enter. It reads the image as loaded (and cropped), before any sharpening, and never changes it.

Does it work with colour (one-shot colour) images?

Yes, experimentally: each colour channel is sharpened and cleaned separately, so it takes about three times as long. Mono images are the main use for now.

Is my image uploaded anywhere?

No. Everything runs on your own computer, and your images are never uploaded. StellAcuity goes online to activate your licence key and, unless you switch it off, to check for a newer version at start-up.

How does the trial work?

The full program runs for 7 days from the first time you start it. You can sharpen, remove stars, reduce noise and compare as much as you like, and save up to 3 finished results. After that it asks for a licence key; your settings and images are untouched.

About

StellAcuity is made by John Hawkes, an amateur astrophotographer in the United Kingdom.

John studied theoretical physics at the University of Kent in the United Kingdom, then took a Master of Science in software engineering, specialising in parallel computing. He spent the next twenty years as a software engineer, on work as diverse as computational fluid dynamics, futures-market modelling, operating systems for transputers and a wide range of business applications, before retraining for a career in medicine, where he became a lead MRI radiographer - a working life spent between physics, high-performance software and the art of getting clear images out of difficult data.

Under the night sky, he images from an observatory he built himself in his own garden, where his main instrument is a 12-inch Ritchey-Chrétien telescope and much of the equipment runs on firmware he has written himself. Every example on this page is his own data - the same kind of data you bring home from a night under the stars, with all its soft stars, seeing and corner aberrations.

StellAcuity is the result of a long effort to recover the sharpness that the atmosphere and optics take away, while staying true to the data. It is developed and supported by John himself, so questions, suggestions and examples of your own results are always welcome at support@stellacuity.com.
